# Psychology and measurement: annotated research

Reviewed 2026-11-03. Sources inform the concepts; they have not validated our custom questions, rubric, verifier or individual trend results.

1. **Task-specific confidence / self-efficacy.** [Bandura, Guide for Constructing Self-Efficacy Scales](https://bpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/websites.uta.edu/dist/1/4994/files/2021/07/Guide-for-Constructing-Self-Efficacy-Scales-Bandura2006.pdf) distinguishes belief in capability for particular demands from a general personality label. This motivates the specific pre-talk question; our single 0–10 question is custom, not Bandura's validated instrument and not a proficiency score.
2. **Speaking self-efficacy.** [Wang and Sun (2024), Development and validation of scales for speaking self-efficacy](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0297517) developed a 15-item English-as-a-foreign-language speaking self-efficacy scale across four dimensions. Its validation involved Mandarin-speaking university students in China; its relevance to other people and settings requires care. The paper discusses an earlier 12-item English public speaking self-efficacy instrument; do not confuse that prior instrument with Wang and Sun's 15-item scale. Optional future use requires the exact original version, conditions, scoring and permission where relevant. Our custom question is not the scale.
3. **Public-speaking anxiety.** [Mörtberg et al. (2018), Psychometric Properties of the Personal Report of Public Speaking Anxiety](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30595793/) studied a shortened PRPSA-18 with 273 Swedish university students. It concerns fear/apprehension, not the quality of a particular speech or a universal diagnostic threshold. Our optional one-item nervousness check is not PRPSA-18 and must not be scored or interpreted as it. An optional formal instrument requires exact administration, scoring and appropriate permissions.
4. **Feelings and performance can differ.** [Hofmann and DiBartolo (2000)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1475776/) developed a questionnaire specifically about self-statements during speaking. [A study of anticipatory anxiety and performance](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33034845/) found a dissociation in its virtual public-speaking setting. We therefore track self-reports and performance separately; neither study establishes how they relate for this user.
5. **Raters disagree.** [Joe et al. (2015), ETS public-speaking scoring study](https://doi.org/10.1002/ets2.12083) examined 17 speakers and several raters. Agreement differed considerably by dimension, with weak agreement on word choice in that sample. We use visible anchors, keep judgments separate, and record who assessed each; two AI judgments do not guarantee independence or accuracy.
6. **Tasks affect results.** [Roohr et al. (2018), ETS task-design investigation](https://www.ets.org/research/policy_research_reports/publications/report/2018/jyxa.html) studied webcam presentations from eight college students and found task type and length affected scoring reliability. This small preliminary study supports multiple comparable prompts, not a guarantee that three one-minute recordings are reliable.
7. **Situations matter.** [Actual vs simulated public speaking experiment](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24142085/) found greater physiological responses in actual public speaking in its studied setting. Solo camera results therefore should not be assumed to represent a live high-stakes situation.

Additional language proficiency frameworks and source limitations are in [the earlier source collection](../docs/evaluation/sources.md). Original instruments have distinct constructs, populations, response scales and terms; do not mix their items or copy cutoffs into our personal tracker. Nothing here is a mental-health diagnosis.
