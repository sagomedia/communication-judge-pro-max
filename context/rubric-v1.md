# Custom speaking and presentation rubric — v1.0

This rubric is a proposed coaching instrument inspired by published language frameworks and presentation coaching; its anchors and scores are our own and are unvalidated. It is not EF SET, IELTS, or an official CEFR rating. Rate the performance captured, never the person's intelligence, personality, accent, or confidence. Do not average the criteria into an overall percentage or rank until there is sufficient evidence that doing so helps.

## Scoring rules

Use the closest whole-number anchor for the complete sample: **1 = major difficulty**, **2 = developing**, **3 = mostly effective**, **4 = consistently effective for this task**. Ratings depend on the stated goal, audience and speech opportunity. One isolated mistake does not define the whole recording. Cite at least one positive or limiting example per rated criterion; for a rating of 1 or 4, seek an additional example or explicitly note limited coverage. Use `not_assessed` when the recording or context cannot support a defensible rating. Do not infer absence from a truncated recording.

The criteria are related but separate: the same passage can inform more than one only when it demonstrates each claim. Prioritize by impact on the user's stated goal, recurrence and ease of practice, not solely by lowest score.

## Presentation effectiveness

| Criterion and minimum input | 1 — major difficulty | 2 — developing | 3 — mostly effective | 4 — consistently effective |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Main point and purpose**: enough speech plus prompt or stated goal | The intended point cannot be identified even in context. | A point is present but becomes clear only after substantial inference or late clarification. | The point can be identified and mostly relates to the task. | The point is easy to identify and the talk consistently serves it. |
| **Structure and transitions**: enough of the full talk | Ideas are difficult to follow and their relationship is unclear. | Some structure exists but ideas jump or repeat without clear links. | An understandable sequence with some useful transitions; a section may wander. | Opening, development and close fit the task; transitions make relationships clear. |
| **Support and examples**: a task where explanation or persuasion calls for support | Main claims lack needed explanation, leaving the point difficult to understand. | Some support is attempted but its connection to the point is unclear or thin. | Relevant reasons, examples or explanations support the main point, with minor gaps. | Well-chosen support makes the point clear and its connection is explicit where needed. |
| **Audience and task fit**: speaker provides intended audience and goal | Framing or unexplained assumptions consistently prevent the intended audience following the point. | The goal or audience is partly addressed, but key context is missing or poorly matched. | Framing and examples generally suit the described audience and goal. | The amount of context, examples and language consistently serve that specific audience and goal. |

For a short answer that does not require a formal introduction, conclusion, or multiple examples, judge whether its structure suits the actual task. Do not mechanically require a template in every speech.

## Spoken English and delivery

| Criterion and minimum input | 1 — major difficulty | 2 — developing | 3 — mostly effective | 4 — consistently effective |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Fluency and continuity**: original audio, adequate audible speech | Frequent breakdowns make the ideas difficult to follow. | Noticeable hesitations or restarts sometimes interrupt meaning. | Generally sustained and understandable; some hesitation or self-repair. | Flow supports comprehension throughout, with natural pauses and repairs. |
| **Intelligibility and vocal delivery**: clear original audio | Much of the speech cannot be understood in the recording. | Some sections require substantial replay or effort to understand. | Most speech is understandable; a few passages need replay. | Speech is readily understandable throughout, with pacing and emphasis supporting meaning. |
| **Vocabulary and precision**: enough audible or reviewed verbatim speech | Word choice frequently obscures intended meaning. | Meaning is usually recoverable but repeated imprecise choices require inference. | Vocabulary generally conveys intended ideas; occasional imprecision. | Words and explanations convey the intended distinctions clearly for this task. |
| **Grammar and sentence clarity**: enough audible or human-corrected verbatim speech | Recurring sentence-level issues often make meaning unclear. | Some patterns interrupt meaning or require listener reconstruction. | Meaning remains clear despite occasional nonstandard or incomplete forms. | Sentence choices convey ideas clearly and flexibly for the task. |

Natural spoken fragments, dialects and accented speech are not automatically errors. Offer a correction only when the original wording is actually supported by the audio and the intended meaning can be reasonably established. If either is uncertain, ask the user or omit the correction. Grammar and word-choice observations based on unreviewed ASR must be provisional and cannot be rated above evidence confidence. A model's difficulty transcribing a word alone is not a pronunciation error.

## Conditional observations, not universal scores

- **Pace, pauses and fillers:** optionally describe observed patterns and their effect on comprehension. Speech rate depends on task, language, speaker and sample. Never target zero fillers or use universal cutoffs. Transcript filler counts are incomplete if the recognizer removes them.
- **Visible gestures and posture:** report only visible, context-relevant actions and timestamps if footage is adequate. Example: “At 00:24 you pointed to the slide as you introduced the figure.” Avoid scoring confidence, intent, honesty, emotion, personality, or fixed gaze/gesture quotas. No video means not assessed.
- **Interaction:** only assess a recorded exchange with adequate permissions and speaker attribution. A monologue supplies no evidence of turn-taking or listening.
- **Confidence:** the speaker's own rating is a separate self-report, not a model judgment.

## Evidence sufficiency

As a practical starting heuristic, aim for at least 60 seconds of the target speaker's audible speech; this is not a validated minimum and does not ensure sufficiency. Shorter clips may yield limited observations, but leave broad criteria unassessed when they lack evidence. Audience fit requires a stated audience and purpose, not guesswork. Pronunciation and fluency require the audio itself. If recordings omit the beginning or end, don't judge whole-speech structure as though complete.
